# Recommendations

Four direction picks (biosecurity, synthetic biology, history, poetry), two familiar picks and two stretch picks.

---

### Biohazard — Ken Alibek with Stephen Handelman (1999, ~320pp) · direction
**Why:** This is the direction entry's own named entry point. Alibek was first deputy chief of the Soviet Biopreparat programme before defecting, and he narrates from inside a system that weaponised anthrax and smallpox at industrial scale. It is a decision-failure story in the first person, from a man who has to account for what he built.
**Cites:** "Enter through insider testimony and decision-failure narrative, modelled on *Dereliction of Duty*." · "Ken Alibek's defector memoir fits." · "First-person testimony from inside catastrophe is a top register."
**Risk:** The testimony evidence (*The Choice*, *Wild Swans*) comes from victims, so a perpetrator's partly technical account may not carry the same moral charge.

### The Code Breaker — Walter Isaacson (2021, ~560pp) · direction
**Why:** This enters CRISPR and gene editing through Jennifer Doudna's life rather than through a survey. It stays close to the human-nature questions, especially designer babies and He Jiankui's rogue experiment, where science stops being technical. It is the book the Directions entry names outright.
**Cites:** "Enter through a discoverer's biography, such as Walter Isaacson on Doudna." · "*The Moral Animal* (4) shows science lands when tied to human nature." · "History lands through one life rather than a period or institution."
**Risk:** Isaacson is a genial, measured biographer, and "A biographer with a strong verdict beats a measured one" predicts this could land nearer Massie's 3 than Roberts's 5.

### The Path to Power (The Years of Lyndon Johnson, Vol. 1) — Robert A. Caro (1982, ~880pp) · direction
**Why:** Caro follows one man's hunger for power from the Texas Hill Country to Congress. He prosecutes his subject relentlessly, including the stolen elections and the cultivated patrons, while still rendering him vividly. This moves you from admired great men to a life put on trial, at a length the model says is no obstacle. It also covers the man at the centre of *Dereliction of Duty*, seen decades earlier.
**Cites:** "Robert Caro gives one life carrying institutional power at great length." · "Push from admired lives to lives prosecuted." · "Length is no obstacle, since 900–1,100-page books score 5."
**Risk:** Early-career congressional politics is less world-historical than Napoleon or Mao, so the "power concentrated in one person and judged hard" thread may feel smaller in scale.

### Inferno — Dante Alighieri (c. 1320, ~400pp in translation) · direction
**Why:** This is epic narrative, not lyric: a pilgrim travels through damnation, and every sinner is a moral argument made flesh. *The Great Divorce* is openly modelled on Dante, so the dramatised-argument success has a direct ancestor here. Try Dorothy L. Sayers's translation (a friend of Lewis) or Anthony Esolen's for dense moral commentary in the notes.
**Cites:** "Dante joins epic form to Christian moral argument." · "Epic narrative poetry lands and lyric does not yet." · "Argument dramatised inside a fictional frame is a sweet spot."
**Risk:** The poetry claim rests on evidence the model itself calls thin: "The evidence is Homer (4.5) against Wilde's poems (3), which is thin."

### Demons — Fyodor Dostoevsky (1872, ~700pp) · familiar
**Why:** This is the major Dostoevsky missing from your list. Stavrogin, Kirillov and Verkhovensky each live out an idea (nihilism, self-deification, revolution) until it destroys them. It is also Dostoevsky's prophecy of the twentieth-century revolutionary cell, which joins your anchor author to your totalitarianism thread.
**Cites:** "Dostoevsky is the anchor, with five volumes and none rated below 4." · "Ideas land hardest when a single character must live them out under pressure." · "Twentieth-century totalitarianism recurs: Chang twice, Mussolini, and *Brave New World*."
**Risk:** The first third is satire of provincial liberals told by a gossipy chronicler, and "Earnest moral seriousness beats comic irony" suggests that stretch could drag.

### The Gulag Archipelago — Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn (1973; one-volume abridgement ~560pp) · familiar
**Why:** This is testimony from inside the Soviet camps, delivered in a furious, sardonic, deeply personal voice. Solzhenitsyn is a man arguing with the reader and with himself about how ordinary people become executioners. It deepens the Chang/Mao vein with a witness who is also a polemicist. The 2018 abridged edition is manageable, and the full three volumes are fine if you want them.
**Cites:** "First-person testimony from inside catastrophe is a top register." · "Argument lands best in a combative, personal voice." · "Twentieth-century totalitarianism recurs: Chang twice, Mussolini, and *Brave New World*."
**Risk:** It is structured around a system rather than one life, which brushes against "Hypothesis: thematic history with no central figure fails them."

### Klara and the Sun — Kazuo Ishiguro (2021, ~300pp) · stretch
**Why:** This tests whether your near-absence of contemporary literary fiction is avoidance or dislike. It is a Nobel laureate's novel about an artificial companion who must discover whether a human being can be replaced. It also offers a dramatised entry to the AI direction's question of what humans are for.
**Cites:** "Contemporary literary fiction is nearly absent. Hypothesis: this is avoidance, not dislike, since *Atonement* scored 4." · "Enter through builders written as characters, and through the question of what humans are for."
**Risk:** Ishiguro's restrained, withholding narration could trip "Hypothesis: oblique, impressionistic narration withholds the moral clarity they want."

### Pensées — Blaise Pascal (1670, ~350pp) · stretch
**Why:** These are fragments, so formally they sit close to the Goethe aphorisms you rated 2. But Pascal is combative, anxious and personal, pressing the reader with the Wager and "the heart has its reasons." That makes this a clean test of whether your objection was to aphorism itself or to aphorism without a fighter behind it. It is also a strong Christian case from someone with no less intellectual ferocity than Nietzsche.
**Cites:** "*Maxims and Reflections* (2). Hypothesis: aphorism without a combative persona feels inert." · "They may want the strongest case on each side of the God question rather than a settled answer."
**Risk:** If the problem was the fragmentary form itself, Pascal's persona won't rescue it and this lands near 2.
